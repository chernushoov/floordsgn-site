Floor Restoration vs Replacement

May 2026 · 7 min read

Every floor reaches the year 5+ decision point: continue maintaining, partially restore, fully restore, or fully replace. The trade-off is rarely obvious — restoration is cheaper, faster, less disruptive but may leave underlying problems untreated; replacement is more expensive, slower, more disruptive but resets the lifecycle clock. Skipping the assessment and defaulting to "restoration is cheaper" is the most common reason owners end up paying for both: the failed restoration plus the eventual replacement. This page walks the seven-step assessment-to-decision framework, names the criteria that drive each decision, and ends with the cost-and-downtime accounting that lets owners commit confidently.

1 Step 1 of 7 — Visual

Visual + Surface Assessment

Walk the floor under side-light. Document every visible defect with location grid + photograph. Categorise into:

  • Surface wear: Dulling, scratching, sealer wear, traffic-pattern marks. Cosmetic; usually restoration territory.
  • Surface damage: Pinholes, chips, cracks < 0.3 mm width, isolated spots. Localised restoration territory.
  • Structural damage: Cracks > 0.3 mm width, delamination zones, deep pitting. Replacement or major restoration territory.
  • Substrate failure: Heaving, settlement, hollow zones (tap test). Replacement territory — restoration won't fix substrate.
2 Step 2 of 7 — Performance

Performance Test Assessment

Run three measurements that determine whether the floor still meets its original specification:

  • Pull-off test (sample locations): EN 1542 / ASTM D7234 to verify resin-substrate bond still meets spec. ≥ 1.5 MPa cohesive failure (Mode C) preferred. See pull-off test guide.
  • Slip class verification: DIN 51130 R-class or EN 16165 PTV depending on use case. Should remain at or above design spec. See slip class explained.
  • Surface hardness / abrasion: Visual + scratch test for cure quality + wear assessment.

Results below spec but with intact substrate → restoration candidate. Results below spec with substrate defects → replacement candidate.

3 Step 3 of 7 — Coverage

Damage Coverage Assessment

Map damage extent. The percentage of project area affected drives the restoration-vs-replacement decision:

Damage coverageRecommended approach
< 5% areaSpot repair only — patch individual damage points
5-15% areaLocalised restoration of affected zones
15-40% areaFull surface restoration + spot repair of structural defects
40-70% areaFull system replacement candidate; restoration cost approaches replacement
> 70% areaFull system replacement
4 Step 4 of 7 — Substrate

Substrate Condition Assessment

The substrate determines whether restoration is even possible. Three substrate scenarios:

  • Substrate intact + resin layer wearing: Restoration ideal scenario. Re-coat or polish, system continues.
  • Substrate intact + resin layer delamination: Mechanical removal of resin + full re-pour. Substrate preserved.
  • Substrate damaged (cracks > 0.3 mm, hollow zones, moisture intrusion): Substrate repair required first. Restoration possible only after substrate stabilised; otherwise replacement.

Skip this step and assume substrate is sound — and you'll find out 6 months after restoration when the failure mode returns. ASTM F2170 moisture probe + adhesion pull-off at sample locations are the diagnostic. See substrate moisture remediation.

Cost Comparison

6 Step 6 of 7 — Downtime

Downtime + Operational Impact

Restoration typically requires less downtime than replacement. For active facilities (kitchen, hospital, warehouse), downtime cost can exceed material cost differential.

SystemRestoration downtimeReplacement downtime
Microtopping2-3 days (topcoat re-coat)5-7 days (full re-pour)
Polished concrete1-2 days (densifier or re-polish)3-5 days (full grind + polish)
Epoxy SL3-4 days (topcoat)5-7 days
PU-cement (commercial kitchen)3 nights (spot repair)3-7 nights phased
Terrazzo3-5 days (re-hone + seal)10-14 days (full re-pour + grind)

Restaurant downtime cost: ₪8,000-25,000 per closed day. Warehouse downtime cost: ₪15,000-40,000 per closed day. Hospital corridor: highly variable but always significant.

7 Step 7 of 7 — Decision

The Decision

Combine the six prior assessments. The decision logic:

  • Substrate intact + damage < 15% + cost/year favourable + restoration downtime tolerable → restore.
  • Substrate intact + damage 15-40% + cost difference small → owner's call based on aesthetic preference (restoration may show mismatched repair zones).
  • Substrate intact + damage > 40% → replace.
  • Substrate damaged + any extent → substrate repair first, then restore or replace per other criteria.
  • System at or beyond design life + general degradation → replace, plan upgrade to current spec.
  • Use-case has changed (different load class, hot wash-down, new compliance regime) → replace with system matched to current use case.

Common restoration mistakes

  • Restoring over damaged substrate. The restoration looks fine for 6 months, then failure mode returns. ASTM F2170 + pull-off before commit.
  • Microtopping topcoat re-coat without verifying base layer condition. Re-coat over delaminating microtopping just buys 12 months before the whole system lifts.
  • Spot repair without colour match. Microtopping + decorative systems show repair zones as colour mismatch. Plan re-coat over the whole area or accept the mismatch.
  • Full replacement when restoration would have worked. Over-investment driven by applicator preference (replacement = bigger BOQ). Always assess substrate condition first.
  • Restoration ignoring use-case change. If the use case has changed (e.g., light retail → commercial kitchen), the original system spec no longer fits. Replace with correct system.

Restoration vs replace by system type

  • Microtopping: Topcoat re-coat at year 3-5 is the planned maintenance. Full re-pour at year 12-15 is the expected end-of-cycle event. Beyond year 15-18 with substrate intact, decision is owner-led.
  • Polished concrete: Densifier re-application is the planned 2-3 year maintenance. Re-polish at year 15+ if surface wear visible. Replacement only if substrate damaged.
  • Epoxy SL + quartz broadcast: Topcoat refresh at year 5-7 (planned). At year 10-12, evaluate pull-off + substrate; restoration usually best if substrate sound. Full replacement at year 15+ typical.
  • PU-cement (kitchen/industrial): Designed for 15-20 year lifecycle. Spot repair if minor damage. Full replacement at end-of-life — substrate condition usually requires it.
  • Cement terrazzo (heritage): Re-hone + reseal every 7-12 years can extend indefinitely. Replacement only for substrate damage — heritage cement terrazzo lasts 50+ years.
  • Epoxy terrazzo: Reseal every 5-7 years. Restoration possible until year 25-30; replacement at end-of-life.

Tender language for restoration projects

Restoration projects require their own tender language — different from new-build:

"Floor restoration scope: [topcoat refresh / spot repair / full re-pour] as defined in attached substrate assessment report. Substrate verified intact via pull-off test (≥ [threshold] MPa) + ASTM F2170 moisture probe (≤ 75% RH). Restoration applicator's warranty [term] covering both restoration work + interface with existing floor system. Existing floor warranty status documented prior to restoration; restoration shall not void manufacturer warranty where still active. Surface colour-match approved at sample stage prior to full area work."

Final read

Restoration vs replacement is a year 5+ decision that comes up on every floor. The framework — visual + performance + coverage + substrate + cost + downtime + decision — applies regardless of system class. Run the assessment honestly; the answer often surprises owners who assumed one direction or the other. Restoration extends lifecycle when substrate is sound; replacement resets lifecycle when substrate or use-case has changed. Specify the assessment before committing to either approach.
